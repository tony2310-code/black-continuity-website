Four Girls. One Jamaican Plantation. The History Their Names Reveal.

Black Continuity Publishing Group

Published 27 October 2026

Rebecca was 12 years old.

Mary was 10.

Jane was 9.

Nancy was just 7.

Their names appear together in an 1826 slavery register from Spring Garden Estate in Jamaica. Their shared surname was Gale. That is something we know because it was written down. [1]

Were they sisters? Possibly. The surviving extract cannot tell us for certain.

It is a small piece of a much larger history. But once you know these four names, another question becomes difficult to ignore.

Why does a document preserve their names while telling us so little about their lives?

A Page That Was Never Written for Them

The register was not made to tell Rebecca’s story, or Mary’s, or Jane’s, or Nancy’s. It was a government record of people held in slavery.

In June 1826, two attorneys acting for plantation owner John Rock Grosett submitted the Spring Garden Estate return. It counted 572 enslaved people, including 264 men and 308 women. The National Archives identifies the original record as T 71/161, folio 183. [1]

The surviving extract gives us the four girls’ names and ages. Other entries record names, ages, and categories imposed by the people maintaining the register. These details can help researchers identify people across records, but the page does not tell us what the girls enjoyed, feared, hoped for, or said to one another.

We cannot fill those silences with invented scenes. We can only keep looking.

Nine Years Later, the Paperwork Changes

Our first Journal article followed the money behind abolition. This time, the older register lets us see what that financial process left out.

The next document concerns money.

In 1835, a compensation record connected Spring Garden Estate to an award of £8,429 18s 1d. It named John Rock Grosett and referred to 483 enslaved people. Another line reads “480. Returns for 483.” [2]

The 1826 register had counted 572 people. The later record gives different figures.

What changed between those years?

We do not yet know. The two extracts do not provide a complete account of births, deaths, movements, or changes in legal status. They do not establish whether the four girls appear in the later claim. We must not turn the difference between two numbers into a story the evidence has not told us.

Still, there is something unsettling about the contrast. One document preserves the names of children. Another records an amount of compensation arising from the loss of ownership over human beings.

The Money Has a Trail

The payment record contains another important detail.

It refers to court orders from August 1835 and an entry dated 6 November 1835 indicating that the compensation was paid into court, through the Accountant General, in proceedings involving Grosett and others. [2]

That does not mean Grosett personally received £8,429 in cash. The document shows an award and a route into a legal process. On its own, it cannot tell us who eventually received the money or how it was used.

This distinction matters. A name attached to a compensation claim is not always the last name in the money’s journey.

The financial trail may continue through other records. For the girls, the trail is harder to follow.

What Do We Know About Rebecca, Mary, Jane and Nancy?

We know their names were recorded in 1826. We know the ages listed beside them. We know they shared a surname on a page documenting enslavement at Spring Garden Estate. [1]

We do not know from this extract whether they were sisters. We do not know where they were in 1835. We do not know what became of them after emancipation.

It is tempting to supply the missing details because we want their lives to feel complete. But doing that would replace their history with our imagination.

The National Archives cautions that such records often tell us more about enslavers and administrators than about the people they enslaved. Even so, names in a register can be the starting point for further research. [1]

And that is why these four names matter. They give us people to look for, rather than only figures to calculate.

What These Two Documents Teach Us

The answer to our opening question is in the purpose of the records themselves.

The government wanted a count of people held in slavery. Later, it wanted to process compensation claims. Those administrative purposes explain why the documents are so precise about numbers, ownership and money, yet so limited about personal experiences.

The register still preserves something irreplaceable: the names and recorded ages of Rebecca, Mary, Jane and Nancy. We cannot tell their complete stories from this page, but we can recognize that they had stories beyond what the government chose to record.

That is the discovery. Historical records tell us not only what happened. Their gaps also show whose experiences were considered important enough to document.

Where the Evidence Leads Next

The compensation paperwork points towards court proceedings that may explain what happened to the award. The older registers raise another possibility: searching for the Gale names in later records to see whether more of the girls’ lives can be documented.

We have not found those answers yet. We will not promise a discovery before the evidence exists.

We have answered what these two documents can tell us. The next question is whether another record can take us beyond them.
