A Letter From 1970 Reveals Why Nikki Giovanni Saved Her Story

The rejection, the archive, and the Black poet’s papers that became public in 2026

Black Continuity Publishing Group

Two letters reached Nikki Giovanni on the same day. One was a rejection. The other offered a reason to keep going.

In 1970, a university archivist wrote to the poet Nikki Giovanni, asking whether she would consider placing her papers in a collection. She was already publishing work that spoke directly to Black life and the changes sweeping through America. But she was also receiving rejection letters.

Her reply revealed an unexpected connection. Boston University’s invitation arrived on the very day another rejection reached her. The archivist’s interest gave her a reason to return to the typewriter. [1]

It is a small moment in the surviving record, but it opens a much larger question: Who decides which Black writers’ voices are worth saving?

A Letter That Saw Something Worth Keeping

Howard Gotlieb, who helped establish Boston University’s special collections, contacted Giovanni in May 1970 about building an archive of her work. Giovanni replied with appreciation and some surprise. According to the letter quoted by Boston University, no other institution had made her that offer. [1]

The timing mattered. The request to preserve her papers came alongside a rejection of her writing. One response said no. Another said that her work had a future worth recording.

We cannot know everything Giovanni felt that day, and the letter does not tell us the name of the person who sent the rejection. But the words preserved by the university make one point clear: she told Gotlieb that his interest helped her keep working. [1]

The story is not about a university making her famous. She was already a writer with books to her name. It is about an institution choosing to preserve the work of someone whose full career had not yet unfolded.

What Was Nikki Giovanni Writing About?

Giovanni was one of the important voices of the Black Arts Movement. In 1968, she published Black Feeling, Black Talk and Black Judgement. The Smithsonian’s National Museum of African American History and Culture preserves editions of her early poetry and places her work within a movement that explored liberation, beauty, solidarity, and the possibilities of Black cultural expression. [2][3]

Her poems reached beyond any single subject. She wrote about Black identity, political struggle, relationships, and ordinary life. She also made space for tenderness and humor alongside anger and protest. [2]

This matters when people try to reduce an artist to one famous poem or a few lines shared online. A writer’s work changes over time. The letters, drafts, and conversations surrounding that work can reveal how ideas developed and who helped shape them.

That is why an archive can matter even to readers who have never visited one.

Why Did Her Papers Stay Out of Public View?

Giovanni began sending materials to Boston University in 1971 and continued for decades. The collection grew to more than 185 boxes of writings and other material. [1][4]

There was a condition. Giovanni wanted the complete collection opened to the public only after her death. She died in December 2024. In February 2026, Boston University announced that researchers and other visitors could finally use the papers. [1]

It would be a mistake to assume she hid her story because of fear or regret. The university documents her request about when access should begin, but it does not establish a single private motive for that choice.

What we do know is that she spent more than fifty years placing pieces of her life and work in the care of an archive. That is an extraordinary act of preservation.

What Has the New Archive Revealed?

The collection contains much more than finished poetry. Boston University describes letters, photographs, journals, artwork, drafts, recordings, personal records, and other materials. [1]

An exhibition opened in September 2026 to explore the early part of Giovanni’s career. Among the material were plans and conversations involving two major Black musicians, Nina Simone and Leontyne Price. The university also describes correspondence connecting Giovanni to other Black women writers and artists. [5]

In October 2026, WBUR reported that the archive includes recordings of conversations between Giovanni and Nina Simone from an unfinished biography project. Giovanni also considered a performance project with Leontyne Price, but the reporting does not establish that the performance took place. These records offer a different view of a poet many people know primarily through her published words. [6]

These discoveries do not mean every item has been fully studied, or that the story of each collaboration is settled. They mean researchers now have new evidence to examine and new questions to ask.

What Does an Archive Preserve That a Biography Cannot?

A biography usually tells a story after the major events have happened. An archive can preserve the uncertainty before those events were resolved.

In Giovanni’s case, it holds a letter from a time when she was still hearing no from parts of the publishing world. It also holds evidence of relationships, experiments, and projects that may never have become finished books.

The difference is important. A list of awards tells us that a writer was celebrated. A letter may show us who recognized the value of her words before that recognition was secure.

For Black history, the question reaches beyond one poet. Families, artists, organizers, teachers, and communities have long created records that institutions did not always collect or protect. Every preserved letter can offer another way to hear a voice that might otherwise become only a name in someone else’s account.

Whose Papers Are Still Waiting to Be Found?

Nikki Giovanni’s work will continue to speak through her books. Now her papers can tell other stories, too.

The 1970 exchange with Howard Gotlieb is only one piece of a collection built over half a century. It reminds us that remembering is not automatic. People must decide to keep things, protect them, and eventually make them available for others to study.

So here is the question worth carrying into our next investigation: How many other Black voices are waiting in boxes, family drawers, and forgotten collections for someone to recognize their value?
