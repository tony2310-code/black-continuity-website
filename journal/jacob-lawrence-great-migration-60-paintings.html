The Artist Who Painted Why Black Families Left the South

Jacob Lawrence, the Great Migration, and the stories carried north

Black Continuity Publishing Group

Published 28 October 2026

What would make a family leave everything familiar behind for a city they had never seen?

For many Black families in the American South, the answer involved more than the promise of a job. It could mean a chance to live with greater safety, opportunity, and freedom. It could also mean leaving relatives, neighbors, and a familiar home behind. [4]

Jacob Lawrence knew this history through his own family. His parents were among the Black Southerners who traveled north. As a young man, he found a way to tell the story of that movement without writing a book. [2][3]

He painted it.

Not on one canvas, but across 60 paintings that were meant to be seen together. [1]

A History That Was Already Part of His Life

Lawrence was born in Atlantic City, New Jersey, in 1917. He lived in Philadelphia during part of his childhood and moved to Harlem with his mother and sister in 1930. The migration had shaped his family before he became an artist. [2]

In a recorded interview years later, Lawrence explained that the movement of Black people was something he had known through family and community life. He remembered hearing about newly arrived families and people helping them with necessities. [3]

The story was not distant to him. The Great Migration had already shaped where his family lived and the community in which he grew up.

What Made People Leave?

The Great Migration was not one journey made for one reason.

Black Southerners faced segregation, racial violence, and limited economic opportunities. Some left to find industrial work. Others sought safety, education, and greater room to shape their lives. News from relatives and Black newspapers helped people decide where to go. [4][5]

Leaving could mean separating from relatives and familiar communities. Arrival brought possibilities, but northern cities also had discrimination, difficult housing conditions, and conflict. A train ticket did not make those problems disappear. [4]

Behind each line on a migration map was a personal decision. People were deciding what they could endure, what they hoped to find, and what it might cost to leave.

Sixty Paintings, One Story

In 1940 and 1941, Lawrence created what became known as The Migration Series. He was still in his early twenties. Each panel presented a different part of the story, with short written captions accompanying the pictures. [1]

The paintings move through reasons for leaving, the journey north, and life after arrival. They show crowded stations, workers, people carrying bags, and communities changing. Lawrence painted all 60 panels together, moving from one color to the next across the whole series so the works would feel connected. [1][3]

Together, the paintings show something a single image could not: the reasons for leaving, the journey itself, and the difficulties that did not end at the destination.

One painting focuses on letters from relatives in the North. Another highlights the Black press and the information newspapers carried to communities in the South. [6][7]

These details remind us that the story did not belong to one famous leader. Ordinary families were sharing news, making plans, and finding ways to help one another.

A Letter Could Change a Life

Imagine receiving news from someone who had already moved. What would you want to know?

Was there work? Could your family find a place to live? Would the schools offer your children more opportunity?

These are questions we can ask without inventing what any particular family said. The Library of Congress preserves actual letters sent by people considering a move, along with newspapers that reported on the migration. The records show how information helped shape decisions. [4][8]

Lawrence gave both kinds of information a place in his series. In panel 33, news from relatives helps explain the pull of the North. In panel 20, the Black press helps explain how news and possibilities traveled through southern communities. [6][7]

Those were not small details. Information could give someone a reason to consider leaving and a place to begin looking for help.

What Jacob Lawrence Gave Us

Lawrence did not paint every family, every city, or every experience. No artist could.

What he created was a way to see the Great Migration through human choices. He gave space to hardship, work, hope, uncertainty, and the communities Black Americans built.

His paintings also ask us to look beyond famous names. History can live in an ordinary letter, a newspaper notice, a train journey, or the memory of a family member who made a difficult decision.

Here is the discovery at the heart of his work: the Great Migration was not simply a change in population. It was millions of individual decisions that reshaped American cities and culture. Lawrence made those decisions visible without pretending to speak for every family. [1][4]

Where the Evidence Leads Next

Lawrence showed us that letters could influence a family’s future.

The Library of Congress preserves a 1917 letter from Cleveland Galliard of Mobile, Alabama, to a Black church association in Chicago. It offers a chance to move from a broad historical movement to the words of one person seeking a way forward. [8]

What did that letter ask for, and what can it tell us about the decisions behind the Great Migration?

That is where our next investigation could begin.
